Real Robot Mechanisms

This chapter turns the pieces into real subsystems. A good mechanism subsystem owns its motors and sensors, exposes simple methods, protects itself with limits, and logs enough data to debug problems later.

Most FRC mechanisms are variations of a few common patterns. Once you recognize the pattern, it becomes much easier to choose sensors, control mode, safety limits, and commands.

Mechanism Map

Arms

Rotating mechanisms that move to an angle and fight gravity.

Elevators

Linear mechanisms that move up and down and must be zeroed safely.

Flywheels

Spinning wheels that need stable velocity before shooting.

Rollers

Simple motorized wheels for intaking, indexing, transferring, and ejecting game pieces.

Drivetrains

The robot base. It usually has default commands, odometry, gyro use, and autonomous path following.

Wrists

Small arms mounted on larger mechanisms. They often need absolute encoders and soft limits.

Climbers

High-force mechanisms where current limits, brake mode, and end stops matter a lot.

Actuators

Simple on/off or position devices like pneumatics, servos, and latches.

1. Arms

An arm rotates around a pivot. The code usually commands an angle, and the motor must fight gravity differently depending on the arm angle.

  • Typical control: position PID plus arm feedforward.
  • Useful sensors: absolute encoder, motor encoder, limit switch for calibration.
  • Safety: soft limits, max voltage, current limits, and safe starting position.
  • Common mistake: tuning PID while ignoring gravity, which makes the arm behave differently at different angles.

2. Elevators

An elevator moves in a straight line. It is usually controlled by height, but the sensor may measure motor rotations.

  • Typical control: position PID, gravity feedforward, and sometimes motion profiling.
  • Useful sensors: relative encoder, bottom limit switch, sometimes top limit switch.
  • Safety: zero the elevator carefully, prevent driving below zero, and limit speed near endpoints.
  • Common mistake: forgetting that string, chain, or belt mechanisms can skip or stretch.

3. Flywheels

A flywheel stores energy by spinning fast. For shooters, the important question is not just "is the motor on?" but "is the wheel at the right speed?"

  • Typical control: velocity PID plus kS/kV feedforward.
  • Useful sensors: motor encoder velocity, beam break for game piece detection.
  • Safety: wait until speed is stable before feeding a game piece.
  • Common mistake: shooting as soon as the command starts instead of waiting for atGoal.

4. Rollers

Rollers are the simplest and most common mechanism. They move game pieces through the robot: intake, indexer, feeder, conveyor, or outtake.

  • Typical control: percent output or voltage control.
  • Useful sensors: beam break, current sensing, motor velocity if jams matter.
  • Safety: stop when the game piece is captured, reverse to clear jams, and avoid fighting another roller.
  • Common mistake: having three commands all try to control the same roller at once.

5. Drivetrains

The drivetrain is a mechanism too, but it is special because it is always being controlled during teleop and auto.

  • Typical control: default driving command, closed-loop velocity, odometry, and path following.
  • Useful sensors: wheel encoders, gyro, swerve absolute encoders, vision pose estimates.
  • Safety: current limits, brake/coast behavior, joystick deadband, and alliance-aware autonomous.
  • Common mistake: mixing field-relative and robot-relative directions without noticing.

6. Wrists

A wrist is a smaller rotating mechanism often attached to an arm or elevator. Because the base moves, wrist behavior can be harder to reason about than it looks.

  • Typical control: position PID, often with simple gravity compensation.
  • Useful sensors: absolute encoder or motor encoder with a known zero.
  • Safety: collision zones, soft limits, and safe combinations with the arm/elevator.
  • Common mistake: tuning the wrist alone and forgetting it may hit the robot when the arm moves.

7. Climbers

A climber is usually powerful, slow, and dangerous. It often needs stronger safety rules than other mechanisms.

  • Typical control: voltage control, position limits, and sometimes current-based detection.
  • Useful sensors: encoder, limit switch, current draw, ratchet/latch state.
  • Safety: brake mode, current limits, end stops, and driver confirmation.
  • Common mistake: treating a climber like a normal motor when it can damage the robot.

8. Simple Actuators

Some mechanisms are not closed-loop motor systems. Pneumatics, servos, and latches often have a small number of states.

  • Typical control: set a state like open/closed, locked/unlocked, deployed/retracted.
  • Useful sensors: limit switches or pressure/state feedback if available.
  • Safety: make sure the actuator cannot move at the wrong time in the match.
  • Common mistake: not tracking state, so code does not know whether it thinks the actuator is open or closed.

Subsystem Design Checklist

  1. Define the hardware IDs in constants.
  2. Configure motor direction, neutral mode, and current limits.
  3. Convert sensor units into mechanism units.
  4. Add safe public methods like setVoltage, stop, and atGoal.
  5. Keep dangerous direct motor access private.
